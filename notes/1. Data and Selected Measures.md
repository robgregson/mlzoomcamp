# **Report: Feature Engineering, Selection and Data Preprocessing**

## **1. Introduction**

The objective of the preprocessing stage is to transform the original *Default of Credit Card Clients* dataset into a more informative and suitable representation for machine learning.

The original dataset contains demographic information, credit limits, repayment status, bill amounts and payment amounts covering six months. Rather than relying exclusively on individual monthly observations, additional features have been engineered to capture customer behaviour, repayment patterns and changes in financial circumstances.

The preprocessing strategy has four main objectives:

- Convert categorical variables into numerical features suitable for machine learning.
- Engineer behavioural and financial indicators that may provide stronger predictive signals than individual monthly observations.
- Identify and remove redundant features using statistical correlation analysis.
- Reduce the dimensionality of the dataset while retaining potentially useful predictive information.

The following sections describe the measures selected, the rationale behind them and the feature selection decisions made during preprocessing.

## **2. Categorical Feature Encoding**

Three categorical variables are transformed into binary dummy variables using one-hot encoding.

### **2.1 Gender**

The original `SEX` column is replaced with:

| New feature   | Meaning                |
| ------------- | ---------------------- |
| `gender_male` | 1 if male, otherwise 0 |

Only one binary feature is required because the dataset contains two gender categories. The female category is therefore represented implicitly by a value of zero.

### **2.2 Education**

The original `EDUCATION` column is replaced with:

| New feature      | Meaning                |
| ---------------- | ---------------------- |
| `educ_post_grad` | Postgraduate education |
| `educ_grad`      | University graduate    |
| `educ_high`      | High school education  |

The original dataset contains additional values:

- `0`: Undefined
- `4`: Other
- `5`: Undefined
- `6`: Undefined

These categories are excluded rather than assigned potentially misleading interpretations.

This reduces the number of features and avoids introducing categories whose meaning is unclear. Customers belonging to excluded categories are represented by zeros across the three resulting education features.

### **2.3 Marital Status**

The original `MARRIAGE` column is replaced with:

| New feature   | Meaning |
| ------------- | ------- |
| `rel_married` | Married |
| `rel_single`  | Single  |

The remaining categories, including undefined and other, are excluded.

As with education, customers in excluded categories are represented by zeros across the resulting binary features.

## **3. Derived Behavioural Features**

One of the main objectives of the preprocessing stage is to capture patterns of customer behaviour rather than relying solely on individual monthly observations.

The six repayment status columns (`PAY_1` to `PAY_6`) provide information about repayment behaviour over the preceding six months.

Four additional features are derived from these columns.

### **3.1 No-Spend Months Proportion**

`no_spend_months_proportion`

Calculates the proportion of the six months in which the customer made no new purchases.

The dataset uses a repayment status value of `-2` to indicate that no consumption occurred.

$$
\text{No-spend proportion} = \frac{\text{Number of months with PAY}=-2}{6}
$$

This measure may help distinguish customers who are actively using their credit cards from those who are not.

### **3.2 Payoff Months Proportion**

`payoff_months_proportion`

Calculates the proportion of months in which the customer paid off their outstanding balance in full.

A repayment status of `-1` indicates that the customer paid the previous month's balance in full.

$$
\text{Payoff proportion} = \frac{\text{Number of months with PAY}=-1}{6}
$$

This provides an indicator of consistent repayment behaviour and may be particularly useful when predicting default risk.

### **3.3 Rollover Months Proportion**

`rollover_months_proportion`

Calculates the proportion of months in which the customer carried an outstanding balance forward.

A repayment status of `0` indicates that the customer was making minimum payments or otherwise revolving their credit balance.

$$
\text{Rollover proportion} = \frac{\text{Number of months with PAY}=0}{6}
$$

A higher value may indicate a tendency to carry outstanding credit from one month to the next.

### **3.4 Late Payment Rating**

`late_payment_rating`

Provides a measure of the severity of late payments, considering only months in which the customer was late.

The original repayment status values range from 1 to 8 for increasingly severe payment delays.

The calculation is:

$$
\text{Late payment rating} = \frac{\sum \text{Late payment severity}} {\text{Number of late payment months} \times 8}
$$

The result is normalised to a value between zero and one.

For customers with no late payments, the value is set to zero.

This captures the average severity of late payments rather than simply counting how frequently they occurred.

For example, two customers may both have experienced late payments in three months, but one may consistently have been only one month late while the other experienced more severe delays. This feature helps distinguish between these behaviours.

## **4. Credit Utilisation**

Credit utilisation measures how heavily customers use their available credit. Three features were initially engineered using the six monthly bill amounts (`BILL_AMT1` to `BILL_AMT6`) and the customer's credit limit (`LIMIT_BAL`).

### **4.1 Average Credit Utilisation**

`average_credit_utilisation`

Calculates the average bill amount over six months and expresses it as a proportion of the customer's credit limit.

$$
\text{Average utilisation} =
\frac{\text{Mean of six monthly bill amounts}}
{LIMIT\_BAL}
$$

This provides a relatively stable measure of credit usage rather than relying on a single month's bill.

It may help identify customers who consistently maintain high outstanding balances.

### **4.2 Correlation Analysis and Feature Removal**

Two additional features were initially considered:

- `latest_credit_utilisation`
- `max_credit_utilisation`

However, Spearman correlation analysis identified a very strong correlation between these features and `average_credit_utilisation`.

The two features were therefore removed.

This decision reduces redundancy within the feature set. Retaining several highly correlated measures of essentially the same underlying behaviour could introduce unnecessary complexity without providing a meaningful increase in predictive information.

`average_credit_utilisation` was retained as the representative measure of credit utilisation.

## **5. Credit Balance Trend**

An additional feature is derived using linear regression over the six monthly bill amounts.

The monthly observations are assigned the following time values:

| Bill column | Month |
| ----------- | ----- |
| `BILL_AMT1` | 0     |
| `BILL_AMT2` | -1    |
| `BILL_AMT3` | -2    |
| `BILL_AMT4` | -3    |
| `BILL_AMT5` | -4    |
| `BILL_AMT6` | -5    |

A linear regression is fitted to the six observations for each customer.

### **5.1 Bill Trend Slope**

`bill_trend_slope`

The slope of the fitted regression line indicates the direction and rate of change in the customer's outstanding bill balance.

Interpretation:

- **Positive slope:** Outstanding balances are generally increasing towards the latest month.
- **Negative slope:** Outstanding balances are generally decreasing towards the latest month.
- **Slope close to zero:** Outstanding balances are relatively stable.

This provides an indicator of whether a customer's financial position is improving or deteriorating.

It also captures information that may not be apparent from the latest bill amount alone.

### **5.2 Bill Trend Intercept: Feature Removal**

`bill_trend_intercept` was initially included as a feature representing the estimated bill amount at month zero, corresponding to the latest observation.

However, Spearman correlation analysis identified a strong correlation between `bill_trend_intercept` and `average_bill_amount`.

The intercept was consequently removed to reduce feature redundancy.

The trend slope was retained because it captures a different characteristic: the direction and rate of change in outstanding balances, rather than their general magnitude.

## **6. Average Financial Measures**

Two additional features summarise the customer's financial activity over the six-month observation period.

### **6.1 Average Bill Amount**

`average_bill_amount`

Calculates the mean of the six monthly bill amounts.

$$
\text{Average bill amount} = \frac{\sum\_{i=1}^{6} BILL\\\_AMT_i}{6}
$$

This provides an indication of the customer's typical outstanding balance.

It reduces the influence of unusual fluctuations in individual months and provides a more general measure of credit exposure.

### **6.2 Average Payment Amount**

`average_payment_amount`

Calculates the mean of the six monthly payment amounts.

$$
\text{Average payment amount} = \frac{\sum\_{i=1}^{6} PAY\\\_AMT_i}{6}
$$

This provides an indication of the customer's typical monthly repayment activity.

It may help distinguish customers who regularly make substantial repayments from those who make relatively small payments.

## **7. Repayment Proportion**

`repayment_proportion_average_new`

This feature measures the proportion of the previous month's outstanding bill that was repaid in each of the five available monthly payment observations.

The calculation uses the following relationship:

| Payment column | Corresponding bill column |
| -------------- | ------------------------- |
| `PAY_AMT1`     | `BILL_AMT2`               |
| `PAY_AMT2`     | `BILL_AMT3`               |
| `PAY_AMT3`     | `BILL_AMT4`               |
| `PAY_AMT4`     | `BILL_AMT5`               |
| `PAY_AMT5`     | `BILL_AMT6`               |

This alignment is important because the payment recorded for a particular month relates to the outstanding balance from the preceding month.

For each observation:

$$
\text{Repayment proportion}\_i = \frac{PAY\\\_AMT_i}{BILL\\\_AMT\_{i+1}}
$$

Only positive bill amounts are considered valid observations.

The five resulting proportions are averaged using only valid observations. Missing values are excluded from the calculation rather than treated as zero.

This ensures that customers with fewer valid observations are not automatically assigned artificially low averages.

The feature provides an indication of how much of their outstanding balance customers typically repay each month.

It is potentially useful because two customers with similar outstanding balances may have very different repayment patterns.

For example, one customer may consistently repay a substantial proportion of their balance, while another may make only relatively small payments.

Where no valid observations exist, the resulting average is missing. These rows can subsequently be identified and handled during data quality checks.

## **8. Feature Selection and Dimensionality Reduction**

Following feature engineering, Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to identify features containing potentially overlapping information.

Spearman correlation was selected because it measures the strength and direction of monotonic relationships between variables without requiring a linear relationship or normally distributed data.

The analysis identified three features that were considered redundant.

### **8.1 Features Removed**

| Removed feature             | Correlated feature retained  | Reason                  |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| `latest_credit_utilisation` | `average_credit_utilisation` | Very strong correlation |
| `max_credit_utilisation`    | `average_credit_utilisation` | Very strong correlation |
| `bill_trend_intercept`      | `average_bill_amount`        | Strong correlation      |

The decisions were based on the following considerations:

**Credit utilisation**

Latest, maximum and average credit utilisation all describe different aspects of the proportion of available credit being used. However, the strong correlations suggested that they contained substantial overlapping information.

Average credit utilisation was retained because it provides a more representative measure of credit usage across the observation period.

**Bill trend intercept**

The trend intercept represents the estimated latest outstanding balance from the fitted regression line. This was strongly correlated with average bill amount.

Average bill amount was retained as the more straightforward measure of typical outstanding debt, while the trend slope was retained to capture the direction of change.

### **8.2 Removal of Original Monthly Observations**

Following feature engineering, several original columns are also removed.

| Columns removed            | Reason                                                              |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `ID`                       | Customer identifier with no meaningful predictive value.            |
| `PAY_2` to `PAY_6`         | Replaced by aggregated behavioural indicators.                      |
| `BILL_AMT2` to `BILL_AMT6` | Summarised through average bill amount and trend slope.             |
| `PAY_AMT2` to `PAY_AMT6`   | Summarised through average payment amount and repayment proportion. |

The latest repayment status (`PAY_1`), latest bill amount (`BILL_AMT1`) and latest payment amount (`PAY_AMT1`) are retained.

This approach reduces dimensionality while preserving recent financial information and longer-term behavioural characteristics.

Importantly, removing correlated features does not necessarily improve predictive performance. The effectiveness of these decisions should ultimately be assessed through model evaluation.

## **10. Data Quality and Missing Values**

Data quality checks are performed following feature engineering and feature selection.

The checks identify:

- Missing values (`NaN`).
- Infinite values (`inf` and `-inf`).
- The number of affected rows and columns.

Missing and infinite values require particular attention because they can arise naturally from some of the derived calculations.

For example:

- Credit utilisation can produce missing values where the credit limit is zero.
- Repayment proportion can produce missing values where there are no valid positive bill amounts.

Rather than automatically replacing these values with zero, the preprocessing stage identifies them so that appropriate handling can be considered.

Where a repayment proportion cannot be calculated because there are no valid observations, the corresponding row can be excluded from the modelling dataset if appropriate.

## **11. Overall Rationale and Conclusion**

The feature engineering and selection strategy is based on the assumption that customer behaviour and financial trends may provide more useful indicators of default risk than individual monthly financial observations.

The final feature set attempts to capture five important aspects of customer characteristics and financial behaviour:

1. **Demographic characteristics:** Gender, education and marital status.
2. **Repayment consistency:** Whether customers regularly pay their balances in full, carry balances forward or make late payments.
3. **Repayment severity:** How seriously customers fall behind when they make late payments.
4. **Credit exposure:** How much of their available credit customers typically use.
5. **Financial trajectory:** Whether outstanding balances are increasing, decreasing or remaining relatively stable.

Spearman correlation analysis was used to identify and remove three features that contained substantial overlapping information. This helped produce a more compact feature set without deliberately discarding the underlying behavioural characteristics being investigated.

The resulting dataset provides a combination of original financial information and engineered behavioural indicators. It should therefore provide a useful foundation for training classification models to predict whether a customer will default on their credit card payment in the following month.

The effectiveness of the selected features will ultimately be evaluated through model performance and feature importance analysis. In particular, it will be important to establish whether the engineered behavioural features contribute meaningful predictive information beyond the original financial variables.

**One methodological point worth including in your eventual evaluation:** Spearman correlation identifies monotonic relationships between individual features, but it does not establish that one feature is unnecessary to a predictive model. A feature with high correlation may still provide additional information when combined with other features. Comparing model performance before and after feature removal would provide useful evidence that the dimensionality reduction was beneficial.
